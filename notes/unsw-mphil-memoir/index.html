Vocational School → a Second-Tier University → an MPhil at a QS Top 20 University

Autobiography · 2026-10-23 · Junjie Chen

My hometown is well known across China for its overseas Chinese community. Growing up, I often heard people say, “If you don’t study, you’ll end up going abroad to work.”

Well, I did end up going abroad. Just for study, as it turned out, haha.

In that environment, I didn’t really think education mattered much. I didn’t get into a regular high school after the entrance exam, so I followed the crowd into a secondary vocational school to study computing. Not long after starting, I broke a bone and had to take a year off. When I returned the following year, I went straight back to messing around. In class, I mostly did two things: played on my phone and slept.

What finally made me think seriously about my future was the three months I later spent tightening screws in an electronics factory.

I worked from 8 am to 9 pm every day, with just one hour in total for meals and breaks. That experience really scared me.

It was then that I started looking into ways to continue my education. I discovered the separate admissions pathway for vocational students. After years of barely studying, I finally put my head down and worked hard. A few months of preparation paid off: I placed in the top 30 in the province and got into the best university I could access through that pathway at the time, Xiamen University of Technology, to study software engineering.

When I first arrived at university, I never imagined that I would one day study abroad. My plan was ordinary enough: enter competitions in my first two years, then prepare for the postgraduate entrance exams in my third and fourth. I wasn’t aiming for anything grand. Getting into “福star” would have been enough for me.

Things changed in the first semester of my third year.

Our AI lecturer, C, had returned to China after completing a PhD at UNSW and teaching at UTS. He supervised master’s students at our university. After our first class, he told us:

“If any of you are interested in research, come and talk to me. I have a project in collaboration with UNSW. It could also help with your postgraduate admissions interviews.”

I joined C’s research group thinking I would give it a try. I never expected that we would produce some results, or that I would publish my first paper as first author.

It was an EI-indexed conference paper, so go easy on me, haha. But at the time, gradually turning my work into a paper and then seeing it accepted made me really happy. It felt as though my effort had finally found an answer.

Later, C brought me into a meeting with a professor from UNSW. The whole conversation was in English. I could just about follow it, but when it was my turn to speak, I stumbled and struggled to get my thoughts across. Luckily, I had a project I had built myself and integrated into a working application. The professor really liked it.

Perhaps that conversation was the first time I seriously thought: the world is so big; should I go and see more of it?

I started looking into postgraduate study abroad and gradually learnt the difference between coursework and research degrees. After we talked, C strongly encouraged me to apply to UNSW. He even emailed his own PhD supervisor to ask whether there might be an MPhil opportunity for me.

Unfortunately, his supervisor had no places left, so the idea had to wait.

The following year, my second journal paper was published, and the thought of applying for an MPhil returned. C encouraged me again: “You have research results now. Be bold and start contacting supervisors!”

So I began my search in earnest.

I sent nearly ten emails in total. One academic, A, replied. She had already returned to China to teach and couldn’t take me on, but she kindly recommended B. I contacted B next, only to be politely turned down because of my undergraduate background.

By coincidence, B is now the PhD supervisor of a student from the year above me at my undergraduate university. That student was also influenced by C and went to UNSW for a master’s degree. Now he’s doing a fully funded PhD there. That’s something I want to keep working towards too.

After several setbacks, I started to think that a research degree might really be out of reach. My coursework offer had arrived by then, so I joined a few new-student group chats through Xiaohongshu. While chatting away in those groups, I met Z.

He also wanted to apply for an MPhil. We became comrades in the application process, searching the UNSW website for supervisors and research areas, and encouraging each other to keep going.

In April last year, he successfully found a supervisor. Around the same time, C’s PhD supervisor recommended R to me. But our research interests weren’t a close fit. After about a month of conversations, the contact gradually faded.

The real turning point came during a casual chat with Z.

Half joking, I asked him, “What if I contacted your supervisor?”

He readily asked for my CV and forwarded it to the supervisor. To my surprise, a reply came back quickly. It went something like this:

“I’ve looked at his CV and he seems like a student with a lot of potential. Two students working on NLP have also just joined my group. If he’s interested, I could meet with him this Friday.”

I had just one thought: holy shit, there’s hope!

The meeting went really well. The supervisor was willing to take me on and gave me a verbal offer. My English was still pretty bad, though, so I had to get through a language course first, haha.

Later, I came to Sydney and met A in person. She suggested a completely new research direction, quite different from my previous work, with demanding mathematical requirements.

Suddenly, all the gaps in my preparation were exposed.

I was attending English classes, keeping up with assignments and preparing for exams, while also catching up on maths and trying to understand new research material. There was so much I couldn’t follow, and catching up was slow. That period was rough.

With my weak maths foundation, several of my in-person meetings in Sydney didn’t go very well. Together with the workload from the language course, this put my MPhil application on hold. By the time I finally passed the language exam, A had no places available. If I kept waiting, the next opportunity would be in the second half of 2026.

I had thought that a verbal offer meant I was nearly there. It turned out there were still plenty of hurdles in between.

Then, through another chance encounter, I met F, a senior research student. He introduced me to D, and after two meetings, I finally received another verbal offer.

This time, I quickly finished my RP, or research proposal, and submitted the application. After enjoying a proper two-month break, I started working with the group ahead of enrolment in early March this year.

My current work is interdisciplinary and isn’t quite the NLP I did as an undergraduate, but many of the underlying ideas carry over. Getting started has been a little smoother this time. I have some hopes of my own for my first paper in this new direction. I’ll keep those to myself for now and hopefully come back with good news.

On 24 June this year, I finally received my formal offer.

In August, another conference paper was accepted. I’ll soon be heading to Wuhan for the conference.

Back when I was tightening screws in that electronics factory, I never imagined that I would one day come to Sydney for a research master’s degree, or attend an academic conference with a paper of my own.

From vocational school to a second-tier university, and now to an MPhil, my path has taken a few detours. There have been rejections and delays, and times when I thought I was almost there, only to find I had to wait again. Thankfully, I met many people willing to give me a hand, and I was still willing to keep trying.

Looking back, I’ve come a long way. Looking ahead, there’s still so much to learn and catch up on, and plenty to look forward to.

Finally, my heartfelt thanks to C, D, A, Z, and F, who helped me along the way. Your encouragement, recommendations and support, time and again, helped me gradually get here.
